Privacy Policy — Pause & Prose

Effective date: 4 October 2026

Pause & Prose is an Android app that invites you to read before a distracting app opens. This policy explains, in plain words, what the app does and does not do with information. The short version: your data stays on your phone. We run no servers, we have no accounts, and we cannot see anything you do in the app.

The accessibility service

The app lock works through an Android accessibility service. When you switch apps, Android tells the service the technical name of the app now on screen (for example, com.instagram.android) — nothing more.

  • The service compares that name, on your phone, against the list of apps you chose to lock. If it matches, the prose screen opens.
  • It reads no screen content, no text, no messages, no passwords and no screenshots — only the app's package name.
  • The name is never sent anywhere. There is no "anywhere": the app has no server.

So that one pause covers one visit, the service also holds two things in its memory while it is running: the name of the app you are in right now — a single name, replaced when you move to another app and dropped when you reach the home screen or the screen goes dark, never a list and never a history of where you have been — and a note that an app has been let through, which expires on its own, so that opening a keyboard, a photo picker or a permission prompt inside that app does not ask you to pause a second time. The note is made when you pause for an app, and also when you send a quote to an app from the share sheet or open this policy from Settings, so the pause does not stand between you and something you just asked for.

To tell "you switched to another app" apart from "a keyboard opened on top of the app you were already in", the service asks Android which of your installed apps are launchers, which are keyboards, and which have a home-screen icon. Those lists describe the apps on your phone, not you, and they are worked out afresh each time the service starts.

None of it is written to storage and none of it leaves the phone. It lives in the running app's memory and is gone when the service stops.

You turn this service on yourself in Android's accessibility settings, after the app explains it, and you can turn it off there at any time.

What the app stores on your phone

Everything below lives in the app's private storage on your device. We have no copy of it and no way to read it.

One qualification, because "only on your phone" would not be the whole truth: if you have Android's own backup switched on, Android copies that private storage into your Google account, the way it does for other apps, so a new phone can restore it. That copy belongs to you and your Google account — it is still not something we can see — and you can turn it off in Android's backup settings. It is also why the removed check-in entries are deleted outright rather than left on disk: rows nothing can display would otherwise have gone on being backed up.

  • The first name you enter, used only to greet you.
  • The apps you chose to lock, and how often each one asks.
  • The shelves you chose to read from, and any quotes you added yourself.
  • The lines you kept with the heart.
  • Your pause count, your streak, and how many pauses you took on each day, which is what the grid of days on the streak card is drawn from.
  • How many words the lines you paused on add up to, which the streak card holds up against a famous book. It is only a number: the app keeps no list of the lines you were shown.
  • Whether you bought the app or are in the free trial, and when the trial ends.
  • Whether the app has already asked to send you notifications, so that it only ever asks once.

Deleting it: uninstalling the app, or clearing its storage in Android settings, permanently deletes all of it. There is nothing to delete anywhere else.

Earlier versions: an earlier version of the app kept a daily check-in — the rating you picked and anything you wrote or dictated. That feature has been removed, and updating to this version deletes those entries outright rather than leaving them sitting on disk behind a screen that no longer exists.

The microphone

The quote editor has a microphone button, so you can add a quote by saying it instead of typing it. If you use it, Android asks for microphone permission, and your speech is turned into text on the phone itself using Google's on-device speech engine. Your voice is not sent to us or to anyone; the finished text goes into the quote you are writing and is stored only on the phone. If you never tap the button, the microphone is never requested and never used. (Google Play services may download the speech model once; that download is a model file coming to your phone, not your voice leaving it.)

The camera

You can also add a quote by photographing the page it is printed on. The first time you tap the camera button, Android asks for camera permission. If you say no, the app hands you to your phone's own camera app instead and the photo comes back the same way, so the permission is never needed. You can also choose a photo you already have: Android's photo picker gives the app that one picture and nothing else from your library.

The photo is read on the phone itself by Google's on-device text recognition, and the words it finds go into the quote you are writing, where you can correct them. The photo is not sent to us or to anyone. The app keeps no collection of photos: the most recent one it took sits in the app's private cache until the next one replaces it, and is gone when you clear the app's storage or uninstall it. Android's backup does not copy it. If you never tap the camera button, the camera is never requested and never used.

Sharing a quote

When you share a quote, Android's share sheet hands the words and their attribution to the app you pick, with a line saying they were shared from Pause & Prose and a link to the app's page on Google Play. From there they are that app's to handle. The link is the same on every share and carries nothing about you or what you shared. Nothing goes to us, and the app does not record what you shared or where you sent it.

What uses the internet

None of the writing does. Every quote and every passage in the app is built into it and read from your phone. The app never asks the internet what to show you, which is also why a pause opens instantly and works in aeroplane mode.

Four things do use the network. None of them carries anything you write, say or photograph in the app:

  1. On-device model downloads. Google Play services fetches the speech and text-recognition models once, if you use dictation or photograph a page. That is a model file coming to your phone, not your words leaving it.
  2. Google Play purchases. The one-time purchase and the trial are handled by Google Play. Payment details go to Google, never to us — the app only learns "purchased" or "not purchased" and stores that flag on the phone. Google's own privacy policy governs the transaction. Google's billing library also reports technical details about the purchase flow and the phone to Google — the phone's model, country, carrier, time zone and network type — as part of processing it.
  3. Google's ML Kit diagnostics. The speech and text recognition come from Google's ML Kit, and ML Kit reports to Google how it is being used, so that Google can find faults and see how its features are used. A report says which app and which ML Kit feature and version were involved, what happened (a check, a download, a recognition, an error), the phone's language setting, the language the speech model is set to, and an identifier: a random one made for this installation, and for text recognition, which runs inside Google Play services, an identifier for the device. Each report also carries the same technical details about the phone as the billing library's: its maker and model, its Android version and build, the country and mobile network it reports, its time zone and the kind of network it is on. Reports are queued on the phone and sent to Google over an encrypted connection, and the app has no switch to stop them. Simply opening the quote editor is enough to queue one, because it asks whether dictation is available before it shows the microphone. They carry nothing you write, dictate or photograph: not your quotes, not your voice, not the words recognised in a picture and not the picture. They go to Google, never to us — we cannot see them — and Google says it does not pass them on to anyone else. Google describes this data at <https://developers.google.com/ml-kit/android-data-disclosure>.
  4. Google Play's rating card. Once you have been pausing for a few days, the app may ask Google Play to show its rating card, at most once every four months. The card is Google Play's own: if you choose to rate the app or write a review there, Google Play receives it and publishes it as a public review on the app's store page, under Google's terms. The app is never told whether you rated, what you gave or what you wrote, and you can change or delete a review in the Play Store at any time.

What the app does not do

  • No accounts and no sign-in.
  • No advertising, no ad SDKs, no advertising ID and no trackers. The app adds no analytics of its own; the only usage reports are the ML Kit diagnostics described above, which go to Google.
  • No selling or sharing of personal data, and nothing sent to us — we have no server to send it to. What leaves the phone is the Google Play and ML Kit traffic described above, and nothing else.
  • No reading of notifications, messages or screen content.
  • Notifications: one reminder that your free trial is ending, scheduled on your phone when you start the trial and shown only if you allow notifications. Nothing else notifies you.

Children

Pause & Prose is made for adults and is not directed at children under 13. Regardless of age, the app sends us nothing; the only data that leaves the phone is the Google traffic described above.

Changes to this policy

If the app ever starts doing something this policy does not cover, the policy will be updated first and the effective date above will change.
